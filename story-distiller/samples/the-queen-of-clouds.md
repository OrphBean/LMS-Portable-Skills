# The Queen of Clouds

The train left the city at dusk, its windows smeared with the last of the light. Inside, Mara pressed her palm against the cold glass and watched the towers shrink to tombstones. She was leaving, though she had told no one the real reason. In her coat pocket, wrapped in oilcloth, was a brass key that did not belong to any door she knew.

The conductor passed once, twice, a third time. He was a narrow man with a scarred jaw and eyes that lingered too long on her seat. When he stopped at her row, he did not look at her ticket. "You carry a heavy thing," he said. "The clouds are listening." Then he moved on before she could answer.

That night the train crossed a mountain pass where the fog came in low and luminous, and Mara dreamed of a city suspended above the valleys. In the dream the city was made of silk and bone, and the cloud-folk walked on ropes that hung from nowhere. A queen stood at a window without a wall, and her face was the face of Mara's mother before the sickness took her. The queen held out a key. Mara woke with her own key burning in her fist.

At the platform in the mountain town, the conductor was waiting. He did not ask where she was going. He simply pointed to a stair that wound upward into the cloud, gray steps swallowed by mist. "She has been expecting you," he said. "The debt is old." Mara climbed. The town below grew small, then disappeared, then she was walking on a road made of woven white.

The city was a cobweb of rope bridges and hanging gardens, still and silent, and everywhere the clouds had been painted with faces. The cloud-folk watched her from doorways but did not speak. At the heart of the city, in a hall of hammered silver, the queen sat upon a chair of clouds and waited. She was as she had been in the dream, though older, and she smiled like a wound.

"You have the key," the queen said. "You have always had it. I only let you believe you found it." Mara took out the brass key, and it fit the lock of the queen's throat. "It was never a door," the queen said softly. "It is a debt. Every debt must be paid in memory."

Mara could have done anything then. She could have turned the key and freed the city, or broken it and gone home. Instead she asked the only question that had ever mattered to her. "Who taught you to hold a face I would know?" The queen was silent for a long time. "Your mother did not die," she said. "She chose the sky, and she became cloud-folk, and she sent for you." 

The key turned itself. Memory poured out of Mara like water finding its level. She remembered the kingdom, the war, the choosing, and the forgetting that had been arranged for her safety. It had all been arranged. Only the key had been left with her, heavy and warm, so that one day she might choose to remember.

Mara woke on the train before dawn. The conductor was gone. The mountains were behind them and the plains ahead. In her pocket the key was cold now, and lighter. She opened her hand and looked at it with a stranger's eyes, and then she took it out and pitched it into the dark, where it fell forever, and the train carried her on into a morning that she would have to build herself.

The clouds, far to the south, followed the train for one silent mile and then let her go.
