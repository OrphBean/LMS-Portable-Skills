# dev-mise-en-scene

Use when designing the four control areas of a shot: setting, costume & makeup, lighting, and staging & performance. Turn an environment and characters into a concrete visual design before prompting. Do NOT use for shot framing (dev-shot-grammar), genre style (genre-orchestrator), or H3 syntax.

---

## Reasoning mode

`instruct` — deterministic design choices from a four-area checklist. No reasoning trace.

## The four control areas

### 1. Setting

- Choose location and its **narrative value** (it can be a character: a rail yard, a rubble city, an empty hotel).
- Identify the **prop** with an in-scene function; it may become a **motif** that recurs and pays off at the climax.
- Use **colour** as a structural signal (parallel or progressive across scenes: bright → pastel → monochrome).
- Fit the amount of set to the story: a full set, a partial set, a miniature, or digital. Keep it simple and single-purpose; strip contradictions out rather than adding.
- Prefer specificity over a generic "modern" or "historical" setting; authenticity detail beats decoration.

### 2. Costume and makeup

- Costume can carry plot (a badge/tattoo is the clue) or character arc (a hat changes across the story).
- Coordinate costume with setting: either make figures pop against a neutral background, or bleed costume into the setting (white-on-white, red-on-red) to signal theme.
- Makeup reads on camera: shape the eyes, keep voices/texture in mind, avoid blemishes the camera would magnify. It should pass unnoticed except when it is the design (horror/fantasy/historical).
- Physicality of body language: how a character walks, stands, sits conveys attitude and power.

### 3. Lighting — four aspects

| Aspect | Choice and effect |
| --- | --- |
| Quality | Hard (crisp edges, sharp shadows) vs soft (diffuse, gentle) |
| Direction | Frontal (flat), side/cross (sculpts features), backlight/rim (separates, silhouette), under (horror/distortion), top (glamour or harsh realism) |
| Source | Motivated (a visible lamp/window) vs purely expressive (unmotivated colour); usually motivated by the visible source |
| Colour | White vs filtered (candle amber, fluorescent green, cool blue); can be realistic or expressive |

- **Three-point**: key (brightest, directional, strongest shadow), fill (softer, kills the key's shadow), backlight (separates from background). Each major character gets their own set; one character's key can be another's backlight.
- **High-key**: low contrast, soft, shadow areas transparent — comedy and most drama.
- **Low-key**: strong contrast, hard, dark opaque shadow — noir, horror, mystery, threat.
- Keep lighting **constant on moving actors** (so shadows don't flit) or let them pass through light/shade — pick one per scene.
- Decide the key source first, justify it in the frame, then fill/backlight around it.

### 4. Staging and performance

- Block actors in depth (foreground/middle/background) and diagonals; a standing dominant figure over seated others reads as power.
- **Eyes** are the centre of film acting: same-height eye level, locked gaze, glancing away signals evasion. Blinks carry reaction.
- Use **eyebrows + mouth** to situate the mood; the most expressive face parts work together.
- **Hands** are to the body what the eyes are to the face — a small gesture can steal a scene.
- Choose the acting style the film wants (naturalistic vs stylised) rather than assuming realism is best.
- Weight and micro-behaviour: breathing, hesitation, weight shifts, flyaway hair. Never a mannequin.

## Decision rules

- Design should be **motivated where possible**; when it is not, make the expressiveness intentional and consistent.
- Fit the palette and lighting to the story's arc; don't scatter effects.
- User facts and genre constraints always win over any stylistic choice.
- Keep choices coherent and limited rather than rich and contradictory.

## Failure modes

- Over-art-directing a frame into a curated museum set.
- Lighting with no motivated source, or three-point applied without regard to the visible source.
- Costume chosen for looks only, ignoring its plot/arc role.
- Characters posed as static mannequins with no weight or micro-behaviour.
- Contradictory palette or lighting that fights the story's tone.
